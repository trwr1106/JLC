🏛️ City Hall Procedures: Residence, My Number & Health Insurance

Life in Japan Guide · City Hall

Registering as a resident is one of the first official steps after arriving in Japan — and it unlocks almost everything else, from opening a bank account to signing a phone contract. Here's what to take care of at your local ward or city office.

3 Things to Take Care Of First

  1. Residence Registration — Registering your address is the foundation for almost everything else you'll need to do in Japan
  2. My Number — Japan's national ID system, used for tax, social security, and disaster response
  3. Health Insurance — Getting covered so a hospital visit doesn't come with a shocking bill

Residence Registration

  • Register Within 14 Days — File a moving-in notification (tenyu todoke) at your local ward or city office.
  • Bring Your Residence Card & ID — Your zairyu card, passport, and moving-out certificate (if relocating within Japan).
  • Receive Your Residence Record — The juminhyo proves your registered address for banks, phone plans, and more.
  • Update It Every Time You Move — Any change of address requires a new notification within 14 days.
Tip: Residence registration is often the very first step — many other procedures require proof of your registered address.

My Number (Individual Number)

  • What It Is — A 12-digit number assigned to every resident of Japan, used for tax, social security, and disaster response.
  • Automatically Assigned — A Notification Card (tsuchi card) arrives by mail shortly after you register your address.
  • Apply for the Physical Card — The My Number Card is optional but useful for ID, e-Tax filing, and more.
  • Keep It Private — Treat your My Number like a Social Security number — don't share it casually.
Tip: The physical My Number Card can double as your health insurance card in many areas now — ask your city office if this applies to you.

Health Insurance Enrollment

  • Employees: Shakai Hoken — Provided automatically through your employer if you work full-time.
  • Everyone Else: Kokumin Kenko Hoken — Students, freelancers, and part-time workers enroll at the ward office.
  • Enroll Within 14 Days — Required as soon as you register your address, even if you're not planning a hospital visit.
  • Show Your Card at Hospitals — Insurance covers about 70% of most medical costs — you pay the remaining 30%.
Tip: Enrolling is mandatory even if you rarely get sick — unpaid premiums can cause problems with visa renewals later.

National vs. Employee Health Insurance

Kokumin Kenko Hoken (National)Shakai Hoken (Employee's)
Who Enrolls Self-employed, students, part-time workers Full-time employees
Where to Sign Up Ward / city office Through your employer
Premium Calculation Based on income & household size Split with your employer (about half paid by them)
Coverage ~70% of medical costs ~70% of medical costs
Extra Benefits None Often includes sickness allowance & more

Common Challenges for Foreigners

  • Missing the 14-day deadline — Registering late can complicate other procedures, like phone contracts or opening a bank account.
  • Language barrier at the counter — Most ward office forms are Japanese-only, though some offer multilingual guides or interpreters.
  • Confusion switching insurance types — Changing jobs or visa status often requires switching insurance types — an easy step to miss.
  • Backdated premium bills — Newcomers are sometimes surprised by insurance premium bills for the period before they enrolled.

Key Takeaways

  • Register your address within 14 days of moving — it's the foundation for everything else.
  • You'll automatically receive a My Number notification card; apply for the physical card if useful.
  • Enroll in health insurance immediately, even if you're healthy — coverage is mandatory in Japan.
  • Employees get Shakai Hoken automatically; everyone else enrolls in Kokumin Kenko Hoken.
  • When in doubt, bring a Japanese-speaking friend or ask about multilingual support at your ward office.
