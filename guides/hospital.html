🏥 Visiting a Hospital in Japan: A Guide for Foreigners

Life in Japan Guide · Healthcare

Getting medical care in an unfamiliar system can feel intimidating — but Japan's healthcare is generally excellent once you know how it works. Here's how to get the care you need with confidence.

3 Keys to a Smooth Hospital Visit

  1. Know the System — Understanding how clinics, hospitals, and insurance work together
  2. Find the Right Clinic — Locating English-speaking doctors and specialists near you
  3. Come Prepared — Knowing what to bring makes your visit faster and less stressful

Understanding Japan's Hospital System

  • Clinics (Iin) vs. Hospitals (Byoin) — Small clinics handle everyday care; large hospitals handle specialists and emergencies.
  • Show Your Insurance Card Every Visit — Without it, you'll be charged the full cost upfront instead of the discounted rate.
  • You Pay About 30% — National or employee health insurance covers the remaining 70% of most costs.
  • Referrals for Big Hospitals — Some large hospitals charge an extra fee without a referral letter (shokaijo) from a clinic.
Tip: For non-urgent issues, start at a local clinic — they'll refer you to a specialist or larger hospital if needed.

Finding English-Speaking Clinics

  • AMDA International Medical Info Center — A nonprofit hotline offering medical information and clinic referrals in multiple languages.
  • Hospital Finder Websites — Directories like Japan Healthcare Info list English-speaking clinics by region and specialty.
  • Ask Your Embassy — Most embassies keep an informal list of recommended English-speaking doctors nearby.
  • Bring a Friend or Interpreter — Community accompaniment can make communication far easier during your visit.
Tip: Try searching "[your city] + English speaking clinic" — many clinics near international areas list language support online.

What to Bring & How to Prepare

  • Your Health Insurance Card — Required at check-in for every visit — don't leave home without it.
  • Your My Number Card (If Applicable) — Some clinics and hospitals now accept this in place of the insurance card.
  • Cash or Card for Your Co-Pay — Not all clinics accept credit cards, so bring cash as a backup.
  • A Written List of Symptoms & Medications — Note your symptoms, allergies, and any medications — translated if possible.
Tip: Translation apps with voice input can help during appointments, but writing key details down beforehand avoids surprises.

Clinic vs. Hospital: Which to Choose?

Clinic (Iin)Hospital (Byoin)
Best For Everyday illness, checkups, minor injuries Specialists, emergencies, surgery, hospitalization
Wait Time Short, often same-day Longer, appointment often recommended
Referral Needed No Often, or an extra fee applies
Typical Cost Lower Higher, especially without a referral

Common Challenges for Foreigners

  • Language barrier during consultations — Medical terminology is hard even for fluent speakers — ask if interpretation is available.
  • Not knowing where to go first — Choosing between a clinic and a hospital can be confusing without local knowledge.
  • Cash-only clinics — Some smaller clinics still don't accept credit cards, so keep cash on hand.
  • Understanding your bill — Itemized medical bills can be difficult to interpret without help.

Key Takeaways

  • Start at a local clinic for non-urgent issues; save hospitals for specialists and emergencies.
  • Always bring your insurance card — you'll pay about 30% of the cost with it.
  • Use resources like AMDA or hospital finder sites to locate English-speaking care.
  • Prepare a written list of symptoms and medications before your appointment.
  • Don't hesitate to bring a friend or interpreter for peace of mind.
