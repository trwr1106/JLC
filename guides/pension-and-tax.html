💴 National Pension & Tax Filing for Foreigners in Japan

Life in Japan Guide · Taxes & Pension

Two questions come up constantly in the JLC community: "Do I really have to pay into the Japanese pension system if I'm not staying forever?" and "Do I need to file a tax return?" Both are more manageable than they sound — here's a practical breakdown.

Do Foreigners Have to Join the National Pension (Kokumin Nenkin)?

Yes. If you're a resident of Japan aged 20–59 and you're not already enrolled in Employees' Pension Insurance (shakai hoken / kosei nenkin) through your employer, you're required to join the National Pension (kokumin nenkin) — regardless of nationality. This is separate from health insurance, though both are usually set up at the same city/ward office window.

  • Monthly premium (FY2026): ¥17,920/month, set nationwide by the Japan Pension Service. This amount is revised annually, so double-check the current figure when you enroll.
  • Where to enroll: Your city or ward office, at the National Pension / social insurance counter. Bring your residence card and passport.
  • Struggling to pay? There's an income-based exemption and deferral system (menjo / yuyo) that can reduce or postpone your premiums — ask at the counter rather than simply not paying, since unpaid premiums can affect future benefits and your visa renewal review.

Leaving Japan? You May Be Able to Get Some of It Back

If you paid into the Japanese pension system but won't reach the 10-year minimum needed to receive a Japanese pension later, you can apply for the Lump-Sum Withdrawal Payment (dattai ichijikin / 脱退一時金) after you leave.

Who qualifies:

  • You don't hold Japanese citizenship
  • You paid into National Pension or Employees' Pension for at least 6 months
  • You haven't earned the right to a Japanese pension (10-year minimum) or a disability pension

The catch — deadlines matter:

  • You must file your claim within 2 years of the date you stop having an address in Japan (i.e., after you've moved out and deregistered). Missing this window means forfeiting the payment.
  • The refund amount is calculated from your last premium amount and total months paid, and for National Pension, only up to a maximum of 60 months of contributions count toward the calculation — even if you paid in for longer.
Tip: Because the deadline is easy to miss once you're settled back in your home country, it's worth filing the paperwork with the Japan Pension Service before or immediately after you leave, not months later.

Do You Need to File a Tax Return (Kakutei Shinkoku)?

Most salaried employees in Japan never file a tax return themselves — their employer handles it through year-end adjustment (nenmatsu chosei) in December. But you'll likely need to file your own kakutei shinkoku if any of these apply to you:

  • You're self-employed, freelance, or run your own business
  • You have side income beyond your main job (over roughly ¥200,000/year)
  • You arrived in Japan partway through the year and your employer's year-end adjustment doesn't cover your full situation
  • You have income from outside Japan that's taxable here
  • You want to claim deductions your employer's year-end adjustment doesn't handle — medical expenses over a certain threshold, a home loan deduction in its first year, or furusato nozei donations beyond the simplified one-stop system
  • You left a job partway through the year and didn't start a new one before December 31

Filing period: Typically February 16 – March 15 each year, for the previous calendar year's income. Missing the deadline can mean penalties, so mark your calendar even if you think you might not owe anything.

Where to file: At your local tax office (zeimusho), by mail, or online through e-Tax (Japan's electronic filing system) — e-Tax is generally faster once you're set up with a My Number card.

Tips

  • Set a personal reminder for mid-February each year to check whether you need to file.
  • Keep receipts for medical expenses, donations, and business costs year-round — reconstructing them in February is painful.
  • If you're leaving Japan for good, start your lump-sum pension withdrawal paperwork before you fly out if possible, or immediately after — the 2-year clock doesn't wait.
  • If your situation is anything beyond a simple single-employer salary, it's worth a one-time consultation with a tax accountant (zeirishi) who's used to foreign clients — the cost is usually small compared to the deductions or mistakes at stake.
