🚨 Disaster Preparedness in Japan

Life in Japan Guide · Safety

Japan is one of the safest countries in the world day-to-day, but it's also earthquake- and typhoon-prone. Knowing what to do before, during, and after these events makes a big difference — here's what to know before the ground shakes or the storm hits.

3 Ways to Be Ready

  1. Earthquake Readiness — Knowing what to do in the moment, and right after
  2. Typhoon Season — Preparing before a storm makes landfall
  3. Staying Informed — Getting reliable alerts and information in a language you understand

Earthquake Basics

  • Drop, Cover, and Hold On — Get low, protect your head and neck, and hold onto sturdy furniture until shaking stops.
  • Know Your Exits — Familiarize yourself with exits at home, work, and school before an emergency happens.
  • Watch for Aftershocks — Significant earthquakes are often followed by aftershocks — stay alert afterward.
  • Secure Heavy Furniture — Bookshelves and appliances can be anchored to walls to prevent tipping.
Tip: Practice the drop-cover-hold motion once so it becomes automatic — you won't have time to think during a real quake.

Typhoon Season

  • Track Warnings Early — Typhoon season typically runs from late summer through autumn — monitor forecasts closely.
  • Secure Windows & Balconies — Bring in loose items and reinforce windows before a storm arrives.
  • Stock Up in Advance — Water, food, and batteries can sell out quickly right before a major typhoon.
  • Avoid Unnecessary Travel — Trains are often suspended during severe typhoons — plan around possible delays.
Tip: Charge your phone and portable batteries fully before a typhoon is expected to hit — power outages are common.

Staying Informed

  • Safety Tips App — A free multilingual disaster app from the Japan Tourism Agency with real-time alerts.
  • J-Alert Broadcasts — An emergency warning system broadcast via phone, TV, and speakers nationwide.
  • Know Your Evacuation Site (Hinanjo) — Every neighborhood has a designated evacuation site — find yours before you need it.
  • Multilingual Disaster Hotlines — Several organizations offer disaster information support in multiple languages.
Tip: Save your city's disaster hotline number and evacuation site address in your phone today — not during an emergency.

Earthquake vs. Typhoon: Key Differences

EarthquakeTyphoon
Warning Time Little to none, sometimes seconds Usually several days
Main Risk Building damage, falling objects Flooding, wind damage, landslides
What to Do Drop, cover, hold on immediately Prepare and secure your home in advance

Common Challenges for Foreigners

  • Language barrier during alerts — Emergency broadcasts are often Japanese-only, especially at the local level.
  • Not knowing the evacuation site — Many new residents don't know their designated hinanjo until it's urgent.
  • No disaster kit prepared — Basic supplies are easy to overlook until they're suddenly needed.
  • Unfamiliarity causing panic — Experiencing an earthquake for the first time can be frightening without prior preparation.

Key Takeaways

  • Practice drop, cover, and hold on so it becomes automatic.
  • Prepare for typhoon season in advance — supplies sell out fast right before a storm.
  • Install the Safety Tips app for multilingual alerts.
  • Find your neighborhood's evacuation site before you ever need it.
  • Keep a basic disaster kit at home: water, food, flashlight, and a portable charger.
