⚖️ Refused at the Hospital? You're Not Alone.

A difficult but important topic for foreign residents in Japan

This Is a Real, Documented Problem

In a 2025 support program report from Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), a foreign traveler who spoke only French and Arabic sought urgent care for chest discomfort and swelling. The clinic they first visited had no cardiology department available at that hour, and when it tried to refer them elsewhere, nearby hospitals turned the case away one after another — largely because of the communication barrier, not the medical need itself.

This is not an isolated story.

  • Japan's foreign resident population passed 3.95 million in mid-2025, an all-time high.
  • Government and university research both describe language and communication as a major, ongoing barrier in healthcare settings.
  • Very few hospitals nationwide have a medical interpreter on staff full-time.

Doctors Cannot Simply Refuse You

What the law says:

  • The Duty to Treat (Oshou Gimu) — Japanese law requires doctors to treat patients who request care, unless there is a valid, specific reason not to.
  • Nationality Is Not a Valid Reason — The Ministry of Health has explicitly stated that refusing care based on a patient's race or nationality is not justified.
  • Refusal Is Only Allowed in Narrow Cases — Exceptions are generally limited to non-urgent situations with no prior doctor-patient relationship.
  • This Applies Everywhere in Japan — The duty to treat is a nationwide legal standard, not a regional policy that varies by hospital.
In short: being turned away simply because you don't speak fluent Japanese is not how the system is supposed to work — even if it sometimes happens in practice.

Why It Still Happens (The Real Barriers)

  • No Interpreter on Staff — Most hospitals, especially outside major cities, don't have a medical interpreter available.
  • Fear of Miscommunication — Staff may worry about safely explaining a diagnosis or getting accurate consent without a shared language.
  • Lack of Department Coverage — Smaller clinics may not have the right specialist available at that hour, regardless of language.
  • Uncertainty About Insurance — Some staff hesitate if they're unsure how a foreign patient's insurance or payment will work.

None of this makes it acceptable — but understanding why it happens helps you respond effectively.

What You Can Do in the Moment

  • Ask for a Referral, Not a Refusal — If a clinic can't treat you, ask specifically for a referral to a hospital that can — don't just walk away.
  • Mention Multilingual Support Options — Some hospitals are certified to accept foreign patients — ask if they know of one nearby.
  • Call a Medical Interpretation Hotline — Services like the AMDA International Medical Information Center offer free multilingual phone support.
  • Bring Someone With You — A friend, interpreter, or accompaniment service can make communication far smoother from the start.
Tip: Keep the AMDA hotline number and your insurance card together somewhere you can grab quickly in an emergency.
