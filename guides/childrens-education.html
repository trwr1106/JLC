🎒 Children's Education in Japan

Life in Japan Guide · Family & Education

Helping your child start school in Japan is one of the bigger adjustments a family makes when settling in — from enrollment paperwork to a whole new language and classroom culture. Here's what to expect across the three main pieces: enrollment, language support, and everyday school life.

3 Steps to Getting Started

  1. Enrollment — Registering your child with the local school system
  2. Language Support — Helping your child adjust to a Japanese-language classroom
  3. School Life & Culture — Understanding daily routines and expectations

Enrolling in School

  • Contact Your City or Ward Office — Public school enrollment is arranged through your local municipal office.
  • Required Documents — Your residence card, your child's proof of address, and sometimes prior school records.
  • Age Cutoffs — Japan's school year starts in April — enrollment age is based on your child's birthdate.
  • Mid-Year Enrollment — Children can generally join a public school mid-year if you move to Japan outside of April.
Tip: Bring your child's previous school records, even informally translated, to help place them in the right grade level.

Japanese Language Support

  • JSL Classes — Some schools offer Japanese as a Second Language support for non-native speakers.
  • Adjustment Period — It's normal for children to take several months to feel comfortable in a new language environment.
  • Homework Support at Home — Translation apps and community tutors can help bridge the gap in the early months.
  • Bilingual Support Staff — Some municipalities provide interpreters or support staff for non-Japanese-speaking families.
Tip: Ask your ward office whether interpretation support is available for parent-teacher meetings — many areas offer this for free.

School Life & Culture

  • School Lunch (Kyushoku) — Most public schools provide a set lunch — let the school know about any allergies in advance.
  • PTA Culture — Parent involvement is often expected, including volunteering for school events.
  • Uniforms & Supplies — Many schools have specific uniform and school-supply requirements, especially in junior high.
  • School Events — Sports days, cultural festivals, and field trips are a big part of the Japanese school year.
Tip: Don't hesitate to ask the school directly about PTA expectations — the level of involvement varies a lot between schools.

Public School vs. International School

Public SchoolInternational School
Cost Free (public education) Often significant tuition fees
Language of Instruction Japanese Usually English or another language
Curriculum Japanese national curriculum International curriculum (IB, American, etc.)

Common Challenges for Foreign Families

  • Language barrier for the child — Starting school without Japanese can be overwhelming in the first few months.
  • Cultural adjustment — Classroom norms and social expectations may differ significantly from your home country.
  • International school costs — Tuition can be a major budget consideration for many families.
  • PTA and volunteer expectations — Parents may be expected to participate more than they're used to elsewhere.

Key Takeaways

  • Enroll through your local city or ward office — not the school directly.
  • Mid-year enrollment is possible if you arrive outside of the April school year start.
  • Ask about JSL classes and interpretation support for parent-teacher meetings.
  • Compare public and international school options based on cost, language, and curriculum.
  • Give your child time to adjust — a few months of difficulty is normal, not a sign of a problem.
