🏦 How to Open a Bank Account in Japan (For Foreigners)

Life in Japan Guide · Banking

Opening a bank account is one of the first things most foreigners need to do after arriving in Japan — you'll need one to receive a salary, pay rent, or sign up for a phone plan. It can feel confusing at first, but the process is straightforward once you know what to bring and where to go.

What You'll Usually Need

  • Residence Card (在留カード) — most banks require at least 6 months remaining on your visa, though this varies by bank.
  • My Number card or notification card — some banks ask for this, others don't. Bring it if you have it.
  • A Japanese phone number — many banks now require this, especially online banks.
  • An address in Japan — usually proven by your Residence Card if the address is updated, or a utility bill.
  • A personal seal (印鑑/hanko) or signature — some banks still require a hanko, though many now accept a signature instead.

Requirements can differ between banks and even between branches, so it's a good idea to check the bank's website or call ahead before you go.

Which Bank Should You Choose?

Bank Good For Notes
Japan Post Bank (ゆうちょ銀行) Beginners, new arrivals Widely considered the easiest for foreigners; branches everywhere, including small towns.
MUFG / SMBC / Mizuho Long-term residents Major banks, useful for salary accounts, but paperwork can be stricter for new arrivals.
SBI Shinsei Bank English support Popular with foreigners for its English-language service and online banking.
Sony Bank / Rakuten Bank Online banking Convenient app-based banking, but may require a longer residence history to apply.

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Choose a bank based on your needs (a branch near you, English support, or online convenience).
  2. Gather your documents — Residence Card, My Number (if requested), phone number, and hanko or signature.
  3. Visit a branch — some banks accept walk-ins, others prefer or require an appointment. Arriving early in the day can mean a shorter wait.
  4. Fill out the application form — staff can usually help, though English support varies by branch.
  5. Wait for your cash card — for security reasons, most banks mail the cash (ATM) card to your registered address rather than handing it over on the spot. This usually takes about one to two weeks.

Tips

  • Double-check that the address on your Residence Card matches where you actually live — mismatches are a common reason for delays.
  • If a branch turns you down (this can happen for a variety of reasons), it's worth trying a different branch or a different bank.
  • Bring a Japanese-speaking friend if you're not confident with the language — it can make the process much smoother.
