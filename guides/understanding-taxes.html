🧾 Understanding Taxes in Japan

Life in Japan Guide · Taxes

The Japanese tax system trips up a lot of new residents — not because it's especially complicated, but because a couple of its quirks (like a tax bill that arrives a full year late) aren't obvious until they happen to you. Here's a plain-language breakdown of the three taxes and filing rules every resident should know.

3 Taxes Every Resident Should Know

  1. Income Tax — Withheld from your salary throughout the year
  2. Resident Tax — A local tax based on last year's income
  3. Filing Your Return — How employees and freelancers each settle up

Income Tax (Shotokuzei)

  • Withheld Automatically (Gensen Choshu) — Most employees have income tax deducted directly from each paycheck.
  • Progressive Tax Rates — Japan's national income tax rate increases as your income rises.
  • Tax Treaties — Some countries have agreements with Japan to help avoid being taxed twice on the same income.
  • Dependents & Deductions — Supporting a spouse or family members abroad may qualify you for deductions.
Tip: Check whether your home country has a tax treaty with Japan — it can meaningfully change what you owe.

Resident Tax (Juminzei)

  • Based on Last Year's Income — Your resident tax bill is calculated from what you earned the previous calendar year.
  • Starts in Your Second Year — Many newcomers are surprised by a resident tax bill that only appears after their first full year.
  • Paid to Your City or Ward — Unlike income tax, this is a local tax collected by your municipality.
  • Paid via Payroll or Direct Bill — Employees often have it deducted monthly; others pay in quarterly installments.
Tip: Budget for resident tax starting around June of your second year in Japan — it often catches new residents off guard.

Filing Your Return

  • Year-End Adjustment (Nenmatsu Chosei) — Most employees have their taxes settled automatically by their employer in December.
  • Kakutei Shinkoku (Self-Filing) — Required if you're a freelancer, have multiple income sources, or want to claim extra deductions.
  • Filing Deadline — Self-filed returns are generally due by March 15 for the previous calendar year.
  • Leaving Japan Mid-Year — You may need to file a final return or appoint a tax representative before departing.
Tip: Keep copies of your withholding slip (gensen choshu hyo) every year — you'll need it for almost everything financial.

Employees vs. Freelancers: Filing

EmployeesFreelancers / Multiple Income
Who Files Employer handles it automatically You file it yourself
When December (year-end adjustment) By March 15 (kakutei shinkoku)
What's Needed Little to no action required Income records, receipts, deduction proof

Common Challenges for Foreigners

  • Surprise resident tax bills — A large bill can arrive in year two, based on income you may have already spent.
  • Confusing withholding slips — The gensen choshu hyo is dense and entirely in Japanese.
  • Missing the March 15 deadline — Late filing can result in penalties, even for a simple oversight.
  • Unresolved taxes when leaving Japan — Departing without settling your final tax obligations can cause issues later.

Key Takeaways

  • Income tax is withheld automatically for most employees.
  • Resident tax is billed a year later, based on the prior year's income — budget ahead.
  • Employees are usually settled via nenmatsu chosei; freelancers must file kakutei shinkoku.
  • Self-filed returns are due by March 15.
  • Check for a tax treaty between Japan and your home country to avoid double taxation.
Note: We're not tax accountants — for anything complex, a licensed professional (zeirishi) is worth the peace of mind.
